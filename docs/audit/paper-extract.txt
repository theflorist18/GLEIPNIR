Introduction
Background and Context
The integrity of digital evidence depends on an unbroken, auditable chain of custody (CoC): a documented record of every action taken on an item of evidence from the moment of acquisition through preservation, transfer of custody, and access, up to its presentation in proceedings. A defect anywhere in that record of an undocumented transfer, an unexplained gap, a record that could plausibly have been altered after the fact is sufficient to undermine the evidentiary value of the item. Chain-of-custody integrity is therefore not just an administrative concern but a first-order requirement of forensic practice.
Blockchain technology has been proposed as a structural answer to this requirement, because an append-only, cryptographically linked, replicated ledger makes after-the-fact tampering detectable rather than merely prohibited by policy. Systematic surveys of blockchain adoption confirm both the breadth of this interest and the recurring set of properties that applications demand of the technology. Sunny et al. document application across transportation, finance, government, healthcare, and the Internet of Things, documenting the diffusion of blockchain well beyond its original cryptocurrency setting [4]. A systematic literature review of blockchain-based applications distils six recurring enabling requirements privacy, scalability, auditability, interoperability, visibility, and latency [5]. Three of those six requirements scalability, auditability, and latency are the axes on which an evidence-management system must be evaluated, and they motivate the present work directly.
Within digital forensics, this general interest has been a recognisable research area, blockchain-based chain of custody (B-CoC). Identifying off-chain storage, zero-knowledge proofs, and self-sovereign identity as the principal directions in which the area is still under-developed [1]. The problem this work addresses sits beneath that list: storing every custody event on a single shared on-chain ledger does not scale, because ledger growth is unbounded and per-ledger throughput is capped. Off-chain storage is the principal response, but the candidate architectures of off-chain anchoring and on-chain channel partitioning have not been benchmarked like-for-like on one substrate. This work supplies that comparison.
A permissioned blockchain is the appropriate substrate, because participation, identity, and access must be controlled and auditable by construction. Comparative performance studies consistently find that permissioned platforms, and Hyperledger Fabric in particular, are well suited to such private deployments. Benchmark Ethereum against Hyperledger Fabric using Hyperledger Caliper and report that Fabric achieves lower latency and higher throughput in private settings [11]. Fabric substantially outperforms private Ethereum for invoke-type transactions, reporting roughly five-fold higher throughput and an order-of-magnitude lower latency, while Ethereum scaled better for read-only queries [6]. Broader permissioned-platform comparisons reach compatible conclusions about Fabric's standing relative to Quorum, Corda, and private Ethereum [23], and a Hyperledger-family comparison across Fabric, Sawtooth, and Iroha documents how core parameters govern latency, throughput, and failure rate across all three [20]. These results, taken together, justify building this study on a single Hyperledger Fabric substrate rather than on a public chain or a different permissioned platform.
Problem Statement
The B-CoC literature has convincingly established that a blockchain can preserve evidence integrity. It has not established which architectural variant an institution should deploy once scalability, throughput, and ledger growth are treated as constraints rather than as afterthoughts. Three concrete gaps follow from this.
Architectural comparability gap. 
The principal architectural ideas relevant to B-CoC have each been studied, but largely in isolation and not benchmarked like-for-like on a single substrate. Direct per-event logging schemes are exemplified by CustodyBlock [8], the private-Ethereum criminal-investigation prototype [13], and the real-time tamper-proof evidence-management architecture [25]. Off-chain batching and Merkle-anchoring ideas appear in the Merklized-transaction scheme [2], the Merkle2 transparency-log system [7], and the hybrid on-chain/off-chain indexing of RollStore [12]. Channel-based and platform-level performance behaviour is characterised by the Fabric/Ethereum and Fabric-family studies [6][11][20][22][23], and the broader scaling and storage trade-offs by the corresponding surveys [3][10]. The comparability gap is therefore that these approaches have seen little exposure.
Institutional scalability gap. 
Most B-CoC prototypes are evaluated at small workloads tens to low hundreds of transactions which is sufficient to demonstrate functional correctness but not behaviour under sustained institutional load. An institution processing continuous case intake requires evidence on transaction density, latency, success rate, and storage growth under load that is orders of magnitude larger.
Standards alignment gap. 
Existing B-CoC papers cite ISO/IEC 27037 in passing without mapping system behaviour to specific clauses, and few position their design against modern forensic frameworks such as ISO/IEC 27043 [18], ISO/IEC 17025 [24], or the SOLVE-IT knowledge base [17]. A B-CoC intended for institutional use should be auditable against such frameworks by construction, and its documentation outputs should be expressible in a form that an accredited laboratory can use.
Research Questions
This work is organised around the following research questions.
On a single Hyperledger Fabric 2.5 LTS substrate and under a common workload and benchmark harness, how do the four architectural variants — Standard, Anchoring, Parallel, and Parallel-Anchored — compare on throughput, latency, transaction success rate, and storage growth?
How do the principal trade-offs play out latency against storage for Anchoring, and aggregate throughput against coordination overhead as the channel count grows?
To what extent can the system's chaincode operation set be annotated against ISO/IEC 27037 and positioned against related forensic standards and knowledge bases to support an accredited laboratory's chain-of-custody documentation requirements?
Scope
The study is bounded as follows. All four variants are implemented on a single Hyperledger Fabric 2.5 LTS substrate, with the state database held constant, Raft ordering, and two peer organisations, so that cross-variant comparison is not confounded by platform or configuration differences. The benchmark instrument is Hyperledger Caliper v0.6.0, pinned. The Fabric configuration files and benchmark definitions are version-controlled and referenced by commit SHA in the methodology, since this is the only credible way to make the comparison reproducible.
A functional smoke test of 10 cases × 10–25 logs validate correctness only and is labelled as such throughout; it generates on the order of one to two hundred events in total and is not a scalability benchmark. A steady-state regime of at least 10³ events per channel is the only regime from which any claim of institutional scalability is drawn. The channel-count sweep is {1, 2, 5}; the 10-channel configuration is deferred unless a multi-host becomes available, because a single core time-sliced across ten channels demonstrates scheduling, not parallelism.
Contributions
The first systematic, single-substrate benchmark of four B-CoC architectures. We implement and benchmark all four patterns like Standard, Anchoring, Parallel, and Parallel-Anchored on one Hyperledger Fabric 2.5 LTS substrate under a common workload and harness. The Parallel-Anchored combination, a per-case channel together with per-case Merkle anchoring to a dedicated anchor channel, is, to our knowledge, unreported in the surveyed B-CoC literature [1] and in the Fabric performance studies examined here [6][11][20][22][23].
A standards-annotated evidence-record schema. A lockb0x-inspired Codex Entry schema whose chaincode operations such as CreateEvidence, TransferCustody, AccessLog, RemoveEvidence are annotated against ISO/IEC 27037 clauses and positioned against ISO/IEC 27043, ISO/IEC 17025, and SOLVE-IT. This is a thin annotation layer and engineering deliverable, not a headline contribution. The schema is designed to support an ISO/IEC 17025-accredited laboratory's chain-of-custody documentation; it is not claimed to make the software "17025-compliant," since ISO/IEC 17025 accredits laboratory competence, not software.
Empirical deployment guidance. Evidence-based guidance on which variant suits which institutional deployment envelope, grounded in the benchmark results and the parameter sweeps.
Literature Review
Blockchain Technology and Its Application Landscape
A blockchain is an append-only ledger of transactions grouped into cryptographically linked blocks and replicated across participants, such that altering a committed record is detectable by any honest participant. The systematic review by Sunny et al., covering 750 application papers, documents the technology's diffusion across transportation, finance, government, healthcare, and IoT, establishing that the blockchain integrity model has been applied far beyond cryptocurrency [4]. Blockchain-based applications into application categories and, more usefully for the present purpose, identifies six enabling requirements privacy, scalability, auditability, interoperability, visibility, and latency [5].
Relation to GLEIPNIR. The enabling requirements identified by Casino et al. [5] are not abstract: scalability, auditability, and latency are exactly the properties an institutional evidence-management system must be measured on, and they correspond directly to the throughput, storage, success-rate, and latency metrics defined in the methodology. This cluster establishes that the problem GLEIPNIR addresses by choosing an architecture that satisfies these competing requirements under load is a recognised first-class concern in the blockchain-applications literature, not a niche concern peculiar to forensics.

Permissioned Blockchain and Hyperledger Fabric
Hyperledger Fabric is a permissioned distributed-ledger platform whose architecture differs in several respects from public, permissionless chains, and these differences are what make it the appropriate substrate for an institutional B-CoC. Identity is managed through Membership Service Providers rather than anonymous key pairs; transaction processing follows an execute–order–validate flow rather than order–execute; and ledger state is exposed as a key–value world state subject to multi-version concurrency control, which rejects at commit time any transaction whose read set has been invalidated by a concurrently committed write. Ordering in current Fabric is provided by a Raft-based ordering service. Crucially for this work, Fabric supports multiple channels, each of which is a fully isolated sub-ledger with its own genesis block, chaincode, membership policy, and block stream, sharing physical infrastructure but not ledger data — the primitive on which the Parallel and Parallel-Anchored variants are built.
Terminology must be fixed precisely here, once, because it licenses the architecture described later. In Fabric 2.5 LTS, channels are created and joined through the channel-participation API (osnadmin channel join), and the older notion of a single ordering-service "system channel" is deprecated in Fabric 2.5 and removed entirely in Fabric 3.0. Accordingly, the dedicated channel to which Merkle roots are anchored in the Parallel-Anchored variant is referred to throughout as an "anchor channel" (equivalently, an "audit channel") — a regular application channel joined by the orderers and a small set of audit peers — and never as a "system channel.”
The security and privacy properties of Fabric have themselves been studied. Brotsis et al. analyse Fabric's attack surface across four components — consensus, chaincode, the network layer, and privacy — and document, among other vectors, denial-of-service against the ordering service, proposing per-component countermeasures and research directions including non-interactive zero-knowledge proofs and post-quantum signatures [21].
Relation to GLEIPNIR. This cluster supplies the platform primitives the design depends on (channels, chaincode, MSP, Raft ordering, world state, MVCC), fixes the channel/anchor-channel terminology that the architecture chapter relies on, and — through Brotsis et al. [21] — anchors the Fabric-specific portion of the threat model, in which the off-chain components and the anchor-client identity are the system-level additions to Fabric's own attack surface.

Performance and Benchmarking of Permissioned Blockchains
A substantial body of work benchmarks permissioned platforms, and this literature serves three purposes for GLEIPNIR: it justifies the platform choice, it establishes the benchmark instrument and its metric definitions, and it foreshadows the contention behaviour that the methodology must control for.
Benchmarking Ethereum against Hyperledger Fabric with Hyperledger Caliper across 500–1,500 transactions at varying injection rates and find Fabric superior in latency and throughput for private IoT-style deployments [11]; Khan et al. compare Fabric against private Ethereum (Geth) across 10–10,000 transactions and 2–16 nodes, reporting roughly five-fold higher throughput and an order-of-magnitude lower latency for Fabric on invoke-type transactions, with Ethereum scaling better for read-only queries [6]; Afif, Schelén, and Andersson compare privateEthereum, Quorum, Corda, and Fabric and document their throughput/latency/scalability trade-offs [23]; and Woznica and Kedziora evaluate Hyperledger Fabric, Sawtooth, and Iroha, documenting how block size, send rate, network traffic, and network size (up to 20 nodes) affect latency, throughput, and failure rate across all three [20].
On the benchmark instrument, several of these studies use Hyperledger Caliper [6], [11], [20], which establishes Caliper as the de facto benchmark tool for Fabric and the source of the metric definitions GLEIPNIR adopts — notably the distinction between offered load (the independent variable) and throughput and the treatment of latency and success/failure rate.
On contention, Thomaz et al. compare Hyperledger Sawtooth and Fabric for smart-contract DApps and report that while Fabric reaches consensus faster, its performance degrades under transaction conflicts — Sawtooth's parallel execution gaining roughly 30% over serial execution under low conflict [22]. This is directly relevant to GLEIPNIR: conflict-driven degradation in Fabric is the MVCC behaviour that the per-record (evidenceId, monotonicCounter) key schema is designed to make structurally avoidable, and the parameter-sensitivity results of [20] motivate the sweep methodology.
Relation to GLEIPNIR. This cluster grounds the decision to build on Fabric [6], [11], [20], [23], fixes Caliper as the instrument and its metric vocabulary [6], [11], [20], and identifies conflict/contention behaviour [22] as a phenomenon the methodology and key-schema design must address rather than absorb as noise.

Blockchain Scalability and On-Chain/Off-Chain Storage
The scalability and storage literature provides the conceptual basis for the Anchoring and Parallel-Anchored variants and for the storage metrics. Hafid, Hafid, and Samih survey blockchain scalability with a two-layer taxonomy centred on sharding (committee formation and intra-committee consensus), situating horizontal-partitioning approaches within the broader scaling design space [10]. Eren, Karaduman, and Gençoğlu survey on-chain, off-chain, and hybrid storage architectures and map the security-versus-performance trade-offs across access-control models, zero-knowledge proofs, fully homomorphic encryption, rollups, and decentralised storage networks [3] — the conceptual backbone for GLEIPNIR's decision to keep evidence binaries off-chain and commit only metadata, hashes, and (for the anchoring variants) Merkle receipts on-chain.
The Merkle-anchoring idea itself has direct antecedents. Davies adds privacy, redaction, and scalability to blockchain data through Merklized transactions, building a secondary per-transaction Merkle tree whose root is committed in the coinbase script and supporting lightweight proof-of-existence [2]; this is the mechanism GLEIPNIR generalises by batching chain-of-custody events into a Merkle tree and committing only the root. Hu et al.'s Merkle2 is a low-latency transparency-log system built from nested two-type Merkle trees forming a multi-dimensional authenticated data structure, with both asymptotic and demonstrated practical improvements over prior transparency logs [7]; it provides the transparency-log lineage for the anchoring variant and a reference point for verification cost. Lin, Gu, and Nawab's RollStore is a hybrid on-chain/off-chain indexing system using a three-stage commit protocol with zk-proofs and an LSM-tree plus Merkle-Mountain-Range hybrid index, with Byzantine-tolerant guarantees and reduced cost and latency [12]; it informs the engineering pattern for the off-chain receipt and index layer.
This cluster also surfaces the most important security caveat of the anchoring design. The off-chain components in [3] and [12] do not carry the integrity guarantees of on-chain storage: the on-chain root (or proof) is authoritative, while an off-chain receipt that is lost or tampered with causes the corresponding verification to fail rather than corrupting the ledger. In chain-of-custody terms this is an availability exposure of the witness, not an integrity exposure of the ledger — but it means the Anchoring variant has weaker end-to-end guarantees than the Standard variant unless the receipt store is itself made robust. This trade-off is stated here and analysed in the threat model; it is not waved away on the grounds that the receipt store is "part of a blockchain system."
Relation to GLEIPNIR. The off-chain storage survey [3] justifies the off-chain design and frames its security trade-offs; the Merklized-transaction [2] and transparency-log [7] work provide the anchoring mechanism and verification-cost framing; RollStore [12] provides the hybrid-indexing engineering pattern and the redundancy idea that mitigates the receipt-availability exposure; and the sharding survey [10] situates per-case channel partitioning within the wider scaling taxonomy.
Chain of Custody and Blockchain-Based Evidence Management
Chain of custody, as a forensic concept, is the documented sequence of acquisition, preservation, transfer of custody, and access applied to an item of evidence. Blockchain-based realisations of this concept form the immediate prior art for GLEIPNIR.
Among the direct per-event logging prototypes, Ahmad et al. propose a real-time, tamper-proof evidence-management architecture on a private Ethereum blockchain that records cryptographically time-stamped metadata for digital and physical evidence and integrates smart locks for physical custody, spanning seizure through court admissibility with verifiable integrity [25]. Tsai designs a chain-of-custody framework for criminal investigations on a private Ethereum blockchain, with administrator, owner, creator, and investigator roles and smart contracts that make evidence creation and transfer traceable and tamper-resistant, validated on a simulated judicial process [13]. Alruwaili's CustodyBlock proposes a distributed chain-of-custody framework on a private blockchain with smart contracts for control, transfer, analysis, and preservation, providing automated, tamper-resistant evidence handling [8].
A distinct strand anchors integrity proofs to an external chain. Jaquet-Chiffelle, Casey, and Bourquenoud build "Horodocs," a tamper-proof, timestamped provenance ledger that anchors multi-level integrity proofs to the Bitcoin blockchain, designed with privacy-by-design and supporting cross- and reverse-verification of evidence [15]. Horodocs is the closest anchoring precedent in the approved corpus: it demonstrates Merkle-style proof anchoring for forensic provenance, but onto a public chain. GLEIPNIR differs in that anchoring is kept internal to the Fabric network with public-chain anchoring deferred to future work; Horodocs is therefore an antecedent for the anchoring idea, not for the Fabric-internal architecture.
The field as a whole is mapped by the bibliometric review of Onyeashie et al., which selects 46 of 672 candidate articles and identifies off-chain storage, zero-knowledge proofs, and self-sovereign identity as the principal open directions [1].
Relation to GLEIPNIR. This cluster defines the chain-of-custody operations the chaincode must implement (acquisition/creation, transfer, access, removal), supplies the immediate B-CoC prior art that the Standard variant represents [8][13][25], provides the anchoring precedent in a public-chain form [15], and — through the bibliometric review [1] situates GLEIPNIR against the field's recognized open directions." 
Forensic Standards, Validation, and Knowledge Bases
GLEIPNIR is positioned against a set of forensic standards and frameworks, and this cluster establishes both their content and the precise, defensible way each may be invoked.
ISO/IEC 17025:2017 concerns the general requirements for the competence of testing and calibration laboratories. Tully and Watson review its application to digital forensics in England and Wales, drawing on Forensic Science Regulator and UKAS accreditation evidence, and find that ISO/IEC 17025 surfaces systemic quality issues and that central ground-truth datasets and validation resources are needed [24]. The essential point for GLEIPNIR is one of scope: ISO/IEC 17025 accredits laboratories — their staff, equipment, methods, traceability, and measurement uncertainty — not software. A B-CoC system can therefore support a 17025-accredited laboratory's chain-of-custody documentation; it cannot itself be "17025-compliant." Claiming otherwise is a category error, and this work avoids it throughout.
ISO/IEC 27043:2015 sets out incident-investigation principles and processes. Makura et al. provide an operational precedent for designing a forensic system against this standard, deploying a prototype agent inside OpenStack cloud instances to achieve digital-forensic readiness while preserving service [18]. This precedent is real but narrow: it is a cloud-readiness mapping, it is a single study rather than an established body of practice, and it does not address chain of custody on a blockchain. It is therefore cited as an operational precedent for ISO/IEC 27043 alignment in cloud forensic readiness and is not over-generalised into a claim that GLEIPNIR is "27043-aligned" by analogy.
Reliability and validation. Stoykova and Franke propose a Reliability Validation Enabling Framework (RVEF) for digital forensics in criminal investigations, a generic, extensible three-level framework spanning technology, methods, and application layers that integrates chain-of-custody documentation as a standard process [19]. RVEF provides the validation lens through which GLEIPNIR's documentation outputs can be understood as part of a recognised reliability framework.
SOLVE-IT is a structured digital-forensic knowledge base modelled on MITRE ATT&CK, organising 104 techniques across 17 investigative objectives with detailed entries (errors and mitigations) for 33 of them at publication, with CASE-ontology linkage and Python tooling [17]. GLEIPNIR's event taxonomy can be mapped to SOLVE-IT, but the resource is community-maintained and in active development; it is therefore treated as a forthcoming standard, and the specific community-repository release tag and date used will be pinned in the standards chapter rather than citing "SOLVE-IT" generically.
ISO/IEC 27037 and CASE/UCO. ISO/IEC 27037:2012 provides guidelines for the identification, collection, acquisition, and preservation of digital evidence, and is the standard against which GLEIPNIR's chaincode operations are clause mapped. Two cautions apply. First, mapping chaincode operations to ISO/IEC 27037 clauses is best framed as a contribution of this work rather than as established practice, and modestly: chaincode events satisfy the documentation requirements of the standard, not the underlying acquisition methodology. Second, regarding the CASE/UCO ontology a claim of "CASE-compatible audit outputs" is only meaningful if the system emits JSON-LD that validates against the CASE ontology (for example with case-utils). Because shipping such a validator is an open decision for this project, no CASE-compatibility claim is made in this draft; the SOLVE-IT mapping stands alone, and the CASE claim will be added only if a runnable validator is delivered.
Relation to GLEIPNIR. This cluster supplies the standards GLEIPNIR is annotated against and — equally importantly — the precise boundaries on how each may be invoked: ISO/IEC 17025 as a laboratory standard the system supports [24]; ISO/IEC 27043 as a narrow cloud-readiness precedent [18]; RVEF as a validation lens [19]; and SOLVE-IT as a forthcoming knowledge base to be pinned by release tag [17].

 Smart-Contract and Chaincode Security
Smart-contract security is a large field, and GLEIPNIR's relationship to it is deliberately bounded. Kushwaha et al. catalogue Ethereum smart-contract vulnerabilities, documenting 24 vulnerability classes with associated attacks and mitigations via a Kitchenham/Petersen systematic review [16]. Ivanov et al. survey smart-contract defences across 133 defence solutions and 539 real security incidents, organised under a five-dimensional classification [9]. Ren et al. build tooling to make Solidity development more secure through real-time secure-code suggestion and multi-tool validation across 47,398 contracts, reporting substantial improvements in suggestion accuracy and in vulnerabilities detected [14]. These works are, however, predominantly Ethereum- and Solidity-centric, whereas Fabric chaincode is typically written in Go and operates under a different execution and trust model; the Fabric-specific security analysis is provided by Brotsis et al. [21].
Relation to GLEIPNIR. This work performs a system-level STRIDE analysis with off-chain batcher, receipt store, anchor-client identity, chaincode lifecycle and defers chaincode-level smart-contract auditing to these platform-level references [9], [14], [16], with Fabric-specific security grounded in [21]. The deferral is therefore deliberate and bounded, not an omission — stated as such so that the security chapter cannot be read as having simply overlooked smart-contract security.

Research Gap
The literature supports a clear synthesis. The blockchain-applications surveys establish scalability, auditability, and latency as recognised first-class requirements [4], [5]. Fabric is a defensible institutional substrate on performance grounds [6], [11], [20], [23], with a characterised security surface [21] and a known sensitivity to transaction conflict [22]. The components GLEIPNIR composes are individually well-founded: off-chain/hybrid storage and its trade-offs [3], [10], [12]; Merkle anchoring and transparency logs [2], [7]; and B-CoC evidence management on permissioned and private chains [1], [8], [13], [25], with public-chain proof anchoring demonstrated for forensic provenance [15]. The forensic-standards frameworks against which an institutional system should be annotated are likewise available, with their scope boundaries understood [17], [18], [19], [24].
What the surveyed corpus does not contain is a like-for-like, single-substrate benchmark of these architectural choices against one another, nor an end-to-end realisation of the specific composition GLEIPNIR evaluates: a per-case Fabric channel combined with per-case Merkle anchoring to a dedicated anchor channel. The three gaps follow directly: an architectural comparability gap (the approaches are studied separately, not benchmarked together on one substrate), an institutional scalability gap (prototypes are evaluated at small workloads), and a standards alignment gap (standards are cited in passing rather than clause-mapped).
The honest positioning of this work is therefore that it presents the first systematic four-variant benchmark on a single Hyperledger Fabric 2.5 LTS substrate, and that the Parallel-Anchored combination has not been reported in the surveyed B-CoC or Fabric performance literature. It is not claimed that "no prior art exists."

Methodology
Overview and Experimental Design
The problem under study is an on-chain one: committing every chain-of-custody event to a single shared ledger as its own transaction does not scale, because ledger growth is unbounded and per-ledger throughput is capped. This work evaluates two architectural responses. Off-chain anchoring aggregates events into Merkle batches and commits only the root on-chain — aggregation over time. Channel partitioning places each case on its own ledger — isolation across space. The two are orthogonal axes, and crossing them yields the four variants studied here, which are the corners of a temporal-aggregation × spatial-isolation design space.
The methodology is therefore a controlled comparative benchmark in which the architectural variant is the only factor allowed to vary. That variant is the independent variable; within the parameter sweeps it is the anchoring batch size (N or K) or the channel count, and within each load characterisation it is the offered load. The state database and chaincode language are held constant throughout, because changing either between variants would invalidate the like-for-like comparison.
Two workload regimes are run and reported separately, and the distinction governs which claims the study is entitled to make. A functional smoke test validate correctness only; only a steady-state regime of at least 10³ events per channel supports any statement about scalability or throughput under load. The design follows the comparative permissioned-blockchain literature: it uses Hyperledger Caliper as the benchmark instrument and adopts its metric definitions [11], and it sweeps the network and workload parameters that prior Fabric studies have shown to be performance-determining [6], [20], [22].

Fabric Network Setup and the Four Variant Configurations
The configuration for all variants shares an identical base: channels are created and joined through osnadmin channel join; chaincode follows the Fabric 2.5 lifecycle from package to install to approve-for-my-org and then commit; the endorsement policy is identical across all channels and variants; and the world state is held in GoLevelDB. The evidence-record schema and the four canonical operations (CreateEvidence, TransferCustody, AccessLog, RemoveEvidence) are as defined in the standard chapter. The variant is selected as a backend configuration without changing the chaincode interface, the client API, or the Caliper workload modules, so that the only difference between variants is how events are written to the ledger.
Standard. 
A single channel carries all cases. Each chain-of-custody event is a single transaction on that channel. This is the baseline and the reference point for storage compression and throughput.
Anchoring. 
A single channel is supplemented by an off-chain Merkle batcher and a receipt store. Events are accumulated off-chain into batches of N; at each batch boundary the batcher computes a Merkle tree over the batch and commits only the root plus minimal metadata to the channel. Each event retains an off-chain receipt holding its leaf hash and the O(log₂N) sibling path. The receipt store does not carry the on-chain integrity guarantee: the committed root is the source of truth, and a lost or tampered receipt causes verification of that event to fail rather than corrupting the ledger; its size is therefore measured and reported separately, and the trust boundary is analysed in the threat model. The receipt-and-index layer follows the hybrid on-chain/off-chain pattern and the redundancy idea documented for such designs [12], with the storage trade-offs framed by [3].
Parallel
The single shared ledger is replaced by one channel per case. Each channel has its own genesis block, chaincode instance, membership, and block stream. Channel provisioning is automated per-channel creation, chaincode install/approve/commit, and per-channel Caliper network configuration. Offered load is distributed across the channels, and throughput is reported both per channel and aggregated. The provisioning policy per case versus per case-family versus per organisational unit is open decision and is stated with the configuration used.
Parallel-Anchored
Each case is provisioned as its own channel and, within each channel, events are batched into smaller Merkle trees of K events. The per-case Merkle roots are committed to a dedicated anchor channel joined by the orderers and a small set of audit peers. Because Fabric chaincode in one channel cannot write to another channel, an off-chain anchor-client submits each Merkle root as a transaction on the anchor channel; the anchor-client's identity, its MSP, and the endorsement policy on the anchor channel are specified explicitly and held constant across runs. The benchmark records per-case-channel throughput, the load the anchoring traffic places on the anchor channel, and the aggregate storage across all channels.
